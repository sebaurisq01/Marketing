Your first business dinner doesn't have to be scary.

A job interview over lunch. A networking dinner with an alumni sponsor. A meal with a client on your internship. This guide gives students with little or no experience a simple, friendly path to feeling confident at the table.

Start the guide

Why does it matter?

When a company takes you out to eat, the food is only part of the event. The people across from you are quietly noticing how you treat the staff, how you handle a tricky dish, and whether you can hold a conversation while eating. Good manners tell them you are careful, considerate and ready to represent their organization. The good news: every one of these skills can be learned in an afternoon.

Before the Meal

Most of the stress at a business dinner disappears with a little preparation. Treat the steps below as your pre-game checklist.

1. Learn the plan

  • Know who is hosting. The person who invited you usually chooses the restaurant, decides when the group sits and orders, and pays the bill.
  • Look at the menu online. Deciding in advance means you won't be flipping pages in a panic while others wait.
  • Mention allergies or dietary needs early. A quick note when you accept the invitation is much smoother than raising it at the table.
  • Find out the dress and the location. Check the route and parking so nothing delays you.

2. Show up on time, and ready

Being late sends the message that the meeting isn't a priority. Plan to arrive about five to ten minutes early. Eat a small snack beforehand so you are not starving; that makes it far easier to eat slowly and talk instead of rushing through the bread basket.

3. Dress one step above the room

If you are unsure how formal the restaurant is, pick the more polished option. Clean, ironed, well-fitting clothes in simple colors are always safe. Keep strong perfume or cologne light, since smells carry across a small table.

4. Put the phone away

Turn it off, or at least on silent, and keep it in your bag or pocket rather than on the table. If you are truly expecting an urgent call, tell your companions at the start and step away from the table to answer it.

If you are the host

Do

  • Pick a quiet restaurant with a menu that suits many tastes.
  • Reserve a table and confirm the day before.
  • Arrive first, and greet guests at the door.
  • Quietly arrange payment with the server ahead of time.

Avoid

  • Choosing a place so loud that no one can talk.
  • Picking somewhere you've never been to impress a guest.
  • Discussing the bill in front of your guests.
  • Ordering for others without being asked.

At the Table

This is where most beginners feel nervous, so we'll take it step by step. Start with the picture below.

Your place setting (tap any item)

Pick a piece

Select any part of the diagram, or use these buttons, to see what it is for and how to use it.

Napkin and seating

  • Wait to be told where to sit if the host has a plan. Then lift your napkin and place it on your lap, folded in half with the fold toward you, once the host does.
  • Use it to dab your mouth, never to wipe your face or blow your nose.
  • If you must leave the table briefly, put the napkin on your chair seat. At the end of the meal, set it loosely to the left of your plate.

Ordering smart

Good choices

  • Mid-priced dishes; the host's suggestion is a great hint.
  • Foods you can cut and eat with a fork or knife, like chicken, fish, or a salad that isn't oversized.
  • Following the host's lead on the number of courses.

Risky choices

  • Messy or hard-to-manage dishes: long pasta, ribs, whole lobster, or French onion soup.
  • Finger foods such as burgers or tacos at a formal meal.
  • The most expensive thing on the menu.
  • Alcohol, unless the host orders some first, and even then, limit it to one.

Eating gracefully

  • Wait for the host (and until everyone is served, when possible) before taking your first bite.
  • Match the group's pace. Nobody should be left with an empty plate while others are still eating.
  • Small bites. It keeps you ready to answer a question without waiting to swallow a giant mouthful.
  • Pass salt and pepper together as a pair, even if someone asks for only one. Place them on the table instead of handing them over.
  • Tear bread into bite-size pieces and butter each piece as you go.
  • Sit tall, keep elbows off the table while eating, and bring the food to your mouth, not your mouth to the food.

Silent signals with your utensils

When you pause, rest your knife and fork on your plate in an upside-down "V" or crossed. When you finish, place them side by side, pointing toward the center of the plate (around the 4 o'clock position), so the server knows to clear it.

Oops moment? Drop a fork? Leave it and quietly ask the server for another. Something stuck in your teeth? Excuse yourself and fix it in the restroom. See someone else make a mistake? Ignore it, it is never polite to point out another person's slip.

Talk, Pay & Follow Up

Conversation

  • Warm up with light topics such as travel, hobbies, school life, or the restaurant itself. Business subjects usually come after orders are placed, and it is the host's cue.
  • Avoid risky subjects: politics, religion, salary, gossip, and complaints about former bosses or professors.
  • Include everyone. Ask open questions, listen, and make eye contact instead of only talking to the most senior person.
  • Don't talk with food in your mouth, and keep your voice at a level only your table can hear.
  • Be warm to the staff. Say please and thank you. Many hosts quietly judge how guests treat servers.

Paying the bill

The host pays. As a guest, don't grab the check or suggest splitting it; just thank your host sincerely. If you are the person who invited, you pay, and the smoothest way is to hand your card to the server privately before dessert, so no awkward moment appears at the table. In the United States, a tip of roughly 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill is customary for good service.

The follow-up

Within a day, send a short thank-you message. An email is fine, and a handwritten card makes you memorable. A good note is three to four sentences: thank them for the meal, mention one thing you enjoyed discussing, and say you look forward to staying in touch.

Sample opening: "Thank you again for dinner last night. I really enjoyed hearing about your first year at the company, and I appreciate your advice on…"

Carreño's Manual: Formal Meals the Classic Way

If you grew up in Latin America or Spain, you may have heard someone say "you haven't read Carreño!" after a lapse in manners. This page explains what that book says about formal lunches and dinners, what you will find on the table, and how to treat the waitstaff and the people sitting with you, then connects those ideas to today's business meals.

Who was Carreño?

Manuel Antonio Carreño was a Venezuelan teacher, musician and diplomat. His Manual de Urbanidad y Buenas Maneras appeared in 1853 and became a huge hit across the Spanish-speaking world; it was reprinted many times and was used for generations to teach children and young adults how to behave in society. Its stated goal is simple: rules of courtesy exist to keep order and harmony among people. Among many topics (cleanliness, visits, conversation, hospitality, travel), it includes guidance about mealtimes, which Carreño treated as one of the places where a person's upbringing shows most.

Note on style: Carreño wrote in the 1800s, so some details sound old-fashioned (his book gives separate roles to men and women, for example). The principles behind them, respect, cleanliness, moderation and attention to others, are still the heart of business dining today.

1. Behaving at a formal meal

Formal lunch

  • Arrive on time and neatly groomed. Cleanliness of body and clothes is one of the book's major themes.
  • Lunch is usually shorter and lighter. Keep conversation friendly and efficient, and don't let it run long.
  • Wait to be shown your seat and sit only after the host (and any older guests) are seated.
  • Dress in clean, modest daytime clothes.

Formal dinner

  • Dinner is the more ceremonial meal: dress a step up and expect several courses.
  • Pace yourself and take modest portions so you can enjoy every course.
  • Conversation can be more relaxed, but stay with pleasant topics that include everyone.
  • Stay until the host closes the evening; leave graciously, thanking the host.

Table manners in the Carreño spirit

  • Keep your body quiet and tidy. Sit upright, keep elbows off the table, and don't lean on it.
  • No grooming at the table. Never use your fingers on your nose, ears, eyes or teeth. If you need to, excuse yourself.
  • Close your mouth when you chew and don't talk with food in your mouth. Avoid noises while eating or drinking.
  • Keep your napkin on your lap and use it only for your lips. If no napkin is provided, the old advice is to keep a clean handkerchief on your knees, but never to wipe your mouth on the tablecloth.
  • Eat in moderation and never show disgust. If you dislike a dish, simply leave it politely; do not comment on it.
  • Wait for the host or hostess before starting, and keep pace with the others.
  • Be thankful. Thank your host for the invitation, in person and afterward.

2. What you will find on the table

A formal table in Carreño's time and today is organized so that each guest has everything needed, arranged in a predictable order:

The cloth and the napkin

A clean tablecloth and a cloth napkin for each guest. The napkin goes on your lap; the cloth is not for wiping anything.

Plates

A plate at each seat, with a bread plate at the left and extra plates brought for later courses.

Cutlery

Forks on the left, knives and spoons on the right, used in order from the outside toward the plate. Dessert utensils sit above the plate.

Glasses

One for water and others for wine or other drinks, placed to the right above the knife.

Shared items

Salt and pepper, bread, serving dishes and small decorations such as flowers, kept low so guests can see one another.

3. Treating the waitstaff (waiters and waitresses)

Carreño dedicates attention to how we treat people who serve us. His message is that those who work for us deserve kindness and respect, and that harshness reflects badly on the person who shows it. In practice:

Do

  • Greet the server and say "please" and "thank you" each time.
  • Call attention with a calm look or a small gesture, never by snapping your fingers or shouting.
  • Explain mistakes or special requests politely and clearly.
  • Let the server finish before speaking, and pause your story when they serve.

Avoid

  • Rude comments, sarcasm or snapping at the staff.
  • Making a fuss about small errors in front of the group.
  • Treating the server as invisible while you talk.
  • Forgetting to tip well for good service.

4. Treating your companions

  • Respect for everyone, especially elders and the host. Let older guests sit and be served first, and offer help with chairs and doors.
  • Serve others before yourself. Offer dishes, bread and drinks to the people next to you.
  • Listen more than you speak. Don't interrupt, don't dominate the conversation, and never contradict someone sharply.
  • Choose topics that unite. Avoid arguments, gossip, personal criticism and anything that may embarrass someone.
  • Be discreet. If someone makes a mistake, pretend not to notice.
  • Silence your phone and give the people at the table your full attention.

Then and now

Carreño's idea

Good manners protect harmony between people.

At a business meal today

Everyone at the table, from your interviewer to the server, forms an impression of you. Courtesy builds trust before a single business point is made.
